A year and a half ago, I said goodbye to Flash once before. That time, Adobe Flash Professional CC was renamed to Adobe Animate CC, marking the end of Flash as a creative tool.

![](/img/in-post/post-f-f-weibo.png)

  

But today, through the Chromium blog post [So long, and thanks for all the Flash](https://blog.chromium.org/2017/07/so-long-and-thanks-for-all-flash.html), I learned that Adobe's official blog announced in [Flash & The Future of Interactive Content](https://blogs.adobe.com/conversations/2017/07/adobe-flash-update.html) that they will stop distributing and updating Flash Player by the end of 2020. This time, it means Flash as a platform has reached its end.

  

In many people's eyes, Flash and HTML5 were purely competitors — we should celebrate the victory of HTML5 and Open Web standards, and curse Flash into oblivion. But in reality, most people have forgotten, or never knew: **HTML5 (strictly speaking, the Web APIs encompassed by its marketing definition) owes a significant portion of its existence to contributions from the Flash platform and the Flash community to web standards.**

As [Flash & The Future of Interactive Content](https://blogs.adobe.com/conversations/2017/07/adobe-flash-update.html) puts it:

> Adobe has long played a leadership role in advancing interactivity and creative content – from video, to games and more – on the web. Where we've seen a need to push content and interactivity forward, we've innovated to meet those needs. **Where a format didn't exist, we invented one – such as with Flash and Shockwave. And over time, as the web evolved, these new formats were adopted by the community, in some cases formed the basis for open standards, and became an essential part of the web.**

When we (companies, users) needed the web platform to host rich interactive content like video and games, but the web platform itself didn't yet have these capabilities, we gave it a new format to meet everyone's needs. That was Flash Player — and that's the objective reason why a proprietary platform and browser plugin could become the de facto web standard for a time.

Fast forward to today: the capabilities the web platform once lacked have, after gaining market and community recognition, been gradually absorbed and refined from Flash into true Open Web standards like HTML5 Video/Audio/Canvas and WebGL. At this point, these proprietary platforms — innovative at birth, serving as "transitional measures" and "shims" — naturally, slowly, ceased to be needed.

**This shouldn't be understood as failure. Rather, they "retired in glory after completing their mission."**

ActionScript 3.0, Flash's official programming language and the sole implementation of ES4, [drove the evolution of the ECMAScript standard and profoundly influenced modern JavaScript](https://www.zhihu.com/question/49170215/answer/114640341);

Adobe Flex, the enterprise development framework for the Flash platform — when I chatted with [@徐飞](https://www.zhihu.com/people/sharpmaster) about it earlier this year, we reminisced about and acknowledged how advanced and mature it was compared to modern web frontend/client development in terms of toolchains, collaboration, compatibility, and UI components;

Adobe AIR, as one of the earliest cross-platform solutions to embed a web-technology Runtime into operating systems or bundle it in executables (borrowing from the JRT concept), can perhaps be seen as a predecessor and successful precedent for Cordova, Electron, NodeWebkit, React Native and the like;

Microsoft IE's proprietary ActiveX technology and its XMLHTTP, as the precursor to XMLHTTPRequest, catalyzed the birth of Ajax and the dawn of the Web 2.0 era;

Google Gears, a browser plugin from 2008 aimed at enhancing web applications — its proprietary APIs were respectively the precursors to App Cache, Web Worker, WebSQL and other standards (or almost-standards);

Cordova/PhoneGap, as the first hybrid solution targeting mobile, served as a polyfill and bridge between web development and mobile devices, accelerating the development of Web Platform Device APIs, and together with WebOS, FirefoxOS, Chrome Apps, Windows Runtime Apps and others, influenced the emergence of Progressive Web Apps;

The years of experimentation with Background Pages and Event Pages in Google Extensions for persistent background computation on the web platform directly informed the API design of Service Workers;

Google's NativeClient and Mozilla's asm.js, in their relentless pursuit of native performance on the web, laid the groundwork for the birth of WebAssembly...

You see, on this road, Flash and its friends were never truly alone.

**"Now that I've watched you grow up, I can leave satisfied."**

**It's as if this were the inevitable law of web technology's evolution,**

**and the Open Web is thus immortal.**

I'm glad that Google Chrome, Mozilla Firefox, and Microsoft Edge could all write something like this:

> Flash helped make the web a rich, dynamic experience, and **shaped the modern set of web standards.**  
>   
> --- "[So long, and thanks for all the Flash](https://blog.chromium.org/2017/07/so-long-and-thanks-for-all-flash.html)" Chromium Blog

  

> Over the years, Flash has helped bring the Web to greatness with innovations in media and animation, **which ultimately have been added to the core web platform.**  
>   
> --- "[Firefox Roadmap for Flash End-of-Life](https://blog.mozilla.org/futurereleases/2017/07/25/firefox-roadmap-flash-end-life/)" Mozilla Blog

  

> Flash led the way on the web for rich content, gaming, animations, and media of all kinds, and **inspired many of the current web standards powering HTML5.**  
>   
> --- "[The End of an Era – Next Steps for Adobe Flash](https://blogs.windows.com/msedgedev/2017/07/25/flash-on-windows-timeline/)" Windows Blog

  

Thank you, Flash.

Thank you, all of you who "retired in glory."
