Today WeChat (China's dominant messaging super-app) blew up my feed again — Mini Programs, the previously rumored "App Accounts."

But this post isn't about the technical details of Mini Programs, nor am I going to speculate (because I know them all too well...),

nor is it about what impact Mini Programs will have on China's internet (product managers will cover that...).

Let's talk about the Web. The Web.

What we casually call "the Web" is actually short for "the World Wide Web."

Just like "H5" (a common but imprecise Chinese shorthand for HTML5), it's an abbreviation of an abbreviation, so after enough shortening nobody remembers what it actually means anymore.

Don't think only Chinese laypeople can't tell the World Wide Web apart from the Internet — American laypeople can't distinguish "Web" from "Internet" either.

And plenty of industry professionals who don't dig deeper aren't much better off. "Web" gets translated so confusingly in Chinese technical articles year after year.

The person who explains this most clearly and cares about it most in the Chinese-speaking world is undoubtedly [@不鳥萬如一](//www.zhihu.com/people/6bec872206d9884cd9535841b6a1f510) (a prominent Chinese tech blogger and podcaster).

For example, in [a post on his Zhihu column](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/20747514) he wrote:

> The Chinese-speaking world has always conflated the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) and the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web). The "open spirit of the Internet" that people keep invoking is really the open spirit of the Web. The openness of the Web is mainly embodied in one thing: **any article on the Web can freely link to any other article via URLs.** If I want to mention the website UbuWeb in my article, I can simply add its URL ubu.com to the word [UbuWeb](https://ubu.com/). You might think this is obvious, but in a WeChat Official Account article you can't do this — you can only add links within WeChat's own ecosystem.

So Ruyì quit WeChat ([Goodbye WeChat](https://blog.yitianshijie.net/2016/02/21/byebye-wechat/)) and also wrote: [WeChat — a De Facto LAN](https://blog.yitianshijie.net/2015/11/16/wechat-de-facto-lan/). As someone who's become increasingly hardcore about the Open Web, I agree with him.

What Ruyì cares about most is probably writing and articles. What I care about more is applications — Web Apps.

A Web App is an evolution of the Web: from providing textual information (hypertext) to multimedia (hypermedia) to providing software application services. A rigorous translation would be something like "applications based on the World Wide Web" — like YouTube, Twitter, Medium, GitHub that you use in your browser. **They can still freely link to each other via URLs, they follow open web standards, and you can use these services on virtually any platform that has a browser — so Web Apps are equally open.**

If you've heard of Google's Progressive Web Apps, what they really stand for is Progressive Open Web Apps — it's just that that's way too long and wordy.

After all, the concept of the Web inherently includes "Open."

(This article isn't meant to advocate for PWA, but if you're interested, feel free to read: [The Next-Gen Web Application Model — Progressive Web App](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/25167289))

If the Hybrid architecture was merely one concession from Web idealism, then the arrival of React Native undoubtedly shattered some people's faith. Then came Weex (Alibaba's cross-platform framework similar to React Native), and then possibly what you're guessing — WeChat.

Watching "developing for native platforms using web paradigms" get more and more popular — even though the supposed beneficiaries are web frontend developers — I don't know if I should be happy.

I'm not saying they're "the wrong technical direction." From a pragmatic standpoint, they're great — they really do solve problems.

**But no matter how much they look like the Web, they are not part of the Open Web platform.**

RN/Weex don't even have URLs (don't bring up Universal Links or App Links — URL and URI are different things).

And WeChat, ever since the JS-SDK, has been a closed ecosystem.

This trend originated at Facebook, but it's more likely to run wild in China.

In the English-speaking world, there are plenty of people who are sensitive and hardcore about this kind of thing. Take Jeremy Keith, who wrote [Regressive Web Apps](https://adactio.com/journal/10708) — he got into a fight with Chrome dev lead Alex over PWA not being friendly enough to URLs, and Alex got so agitated he blurted out:

> so, your choices are to think that I have a secret plan to kill URLs, or conclude I'm still Team Web.

Keep in mind, the reason Alex led Chrome in building PWA was precisely because he couldn't stand Hybrid apps undermining the Open Web.

If Twitter/Facebook did what WeChat does — not even letting you freely link to things — people would probably abandon Twitter, and write furious "Byebye Twitter/FB" posts just like Ruyì did.

Meanwhile in China, people constantly hype about "XX empowering the HTML5 ecosystem," not realizing that most of the time these so-called "HTML5 ecosystems" run directly counter to the Web ecosystem. The contrast couldn't be starker.

I'm starting to ramble.

In this Chinese-speaking world where "HTML5" and "Web" are so wildly misused, I don't even know how to sound the alarm anymore.

All I know is that when the Web can only survive as a "Markup Language" for Native, the Web ceases to exist.

When everyone ignores Web standards and builds their own proprietary stack, the Web's vaunted cross-platform nature evaporates.

As I've written before, and as Chrome product leader Rahul also said at I/O:

The Web's Discoverability, Linkability, Low Friction, Broad Reach — none of these are the essence of the Web. **The essence of the Web is Openness and Decentralization. That was the original vision of the World Wide Web (WWW), and that's the premise upon which all these other properties stand.**

The belief in the Open Web has brought browser vendors back together, and they're asking you:

**Hey, can we make the web great again?**
