## I Don't Understand Either.

For any field I know something about, I can name a long list of things I don't understand. Conversely, for the fields I know nothing about, I can't even articulate what I don't know.

I sometimes think I was more confident — and more eager to share — back when "frontend" was my entire world. The boundary of what I could perceive was maybe 2⁴ = 16, and I felt I'd covered about 4 of it. I could already do the work, and I thought: maybe I'll learn a bit more about JS engines, browsers, how frameworks work under the hood. Maybe pick up some backend. Go full-stack. 4 out of 16 — that's 25% of the universe. Not bad!

Then my knowledge actually grew to 16, and I realized: oh, computer science has *this much more*? Every field runs deep — textbooks cite papers, papers cite more papers, branching out like fractals. The boundary of the unknown grew to 2¹⁶ = 65,536. Suddenly I knew 0.02% of everything. I went from feeling pretty good to feeling like nothing at all. I call this the "exponential growth of unknowns."

All I can do is learn to live with it. Prof. Matt Might's [illustrated guide to a Ph.D.][1] helped me see how small any individual is against the totality of human knowledge. And maybe "growth" is just this: within that ever-expanding set of things you don't understand, choosing which subsets you're still willing to explore.

## Being a Frontend Engineer.

For a long time, "frontend" was both my interest and my career, and the two didn't need to be different. I interned at Alibaba early on, led a team at Weiying before graduating, worked at companies of every size, attended plenty of events. If I'd just kept at it — stayed "a frontend engineer" — I'd probably be doing fine.

But the edges of the unknown kept glowing. A friend once described a certain type of person as "driven by understanding." Maybe that's what it is — I came to engineering sideways (switched from science-track to art in high school, from interaction design to frontend at Alibaba), and I accumulated too many unanswered questions. Or maybe I just wanted to *travel*. There was theory I was curious about, systems programming I hadn't tried, new product paradigms forming on the horizon... The urge to explore the next thing always eventually overtook the comfort of the current one. You hear about a discipline, a civilization, the eye of a storm — but if you don't go see it, you'll never know what it's like.

Recently, people kept asking me "What's the direction for your frontend team?" and I realized I'd stopped thinking that way. The question itself carries the assumption of fine-grained specialization that the market demands. Meanwhile, I was sometimes surprised by how Facebook internally felt like an open-source bazaar — barely managed, gloriously messy. Frontend as a sub-field certainly needs its own infrastructure and expertise. But maybe your career trajectory, your company's org chart, and how software gets built don't all have to be welded together.

What I want isn't just to keep doing what I'm already good at. I want to explore the things I want to try but might not yet be good at — and ideally get paid along the way. Let's call it "technical freedom." So, am I a frontend engineer? Am I not?

## Coda.

Sometimes you need to earn a living. Sometimes you need ideals.  
Would you rather be a hacker-painter, or stand on the shoulders of Hilbert's tenth problem?

Gödel says it's undecidable. Life is no simpler than Peano arithmetic.  
Too many tradeoffs — we can only approximate the shape we want.

These are words from the heart. For the mind, go read more books.  
If any of this gives you a moment of different thinking, that would be the highest reward one carbon-based life form can offer another.
